# Species accounts

Bilingual working draft · 9 October 2026.

## Booted Eagle

### Passage at the Défilé

The Booted Eagle remains a rare encounter at the Défilé. Records, sometimes clustered in successive years, do not establish a definite origin for the birds. Its appearance is chiefly a memorable event within raptor passage.

## Greater Spotted Eagle

### Passage at the Défilé

The Greater Spotted Eagle is a rare migrant, generally passing later than the Lesser Spotted Eagle. Records extend into October and November. The resemblance between these eagles, together with the existence of hybrids, means that some birds cannot be identified with certainty.

## Steppe Eagle

### Distinctive observations

The Steppe Eagle seen in 2013 was an immature bird. Comparison of photographs suggested that it might have been seen previously at Falsterbo in Sweden, without establishing this with certainty.

## Lesser Spotted Eagle

### Passage at the Défilé

The Lesser Spotted Eagle is rare at the Défilé. Its passage generally falls earlier in autumn than that of the Greater Spotted Eagle, from which it can be difficult to separate. Hybrids further complicate identification, so some records are cautiously left as Lesser or Greater Spotted Eagle.

## Lesser/Greater Spotted Eagle

### Passage at the Défilé

This category brings together eagles that cannot be confidently identified as Lesser or Greater Spotted Eagle. The two species resemble one another and can hybridise. Retaining that uncertainty describes passage without arbitrarily assigning birds to a species.

## Golden Eagle

### Passage at the Défilé

Golden Eagles recorded on the move are often immature birds. Regional adults are mostly resident, and separating migration from dispersal remains difficult. A bird steadily following the migration axis is easier to distinguish from local eagles circling above the hills.

## Eurasian Skylark

### Passage at the Défilé

The Eurasian Skylark migrates largely at night. Visible daytime passage at the Défilé may therefore represent a variable fraction of the actual movement. A few very busy days can strongly influence a season’s total.

### Changes in passage

Counts from 1993 to 2019 show no clear trend, despite declines in breeding populations across many parts of Europe. This contrast illustrates the limits of local monitoring for a partly nocturnal migrant. Changes in routes and the proportion of birds remaining near their breeding grounds can also blur the relationship between passage and populations.

## Woodlark

### Passage at the Défilé

The Woodlark often accompanies Eurasian Skylark flocks, usually in smaller numbers. Its smaller size and especially its shorter tail help identify it in flight. Birds detected at the Défilé do not necessarily represent the same populations as those passing on the other side of the Jura.

### Changes in passage

Between the 1990s and 2019, passage showed an initial decline followed by a modest recovery. This resembles the pattern in Swiss breeding birds, but migrants seen here do not necessarily come from that population alone. Similar curves do not establish the birds’ origin.

## Eurasian Goshawk

### Passage at the Défilé

The Eurasian Goshawk remains scarce in the counts. Some birds are resident or move within the region, while birds from farther north may undertake true migration. Distinguishing these situations and finding goshawks within the flow of other raptors requires particular care.

## Osprey

### Passage at the Défilé

The Osprey generally migrates alone and can travel in a wide range of weather. Powered flight allows it to cross without relying entirely on thermals, at very different heights. This broad-front migration limits the concentration of birds at the Défilé.

### Changes in passage

Numbers fluctuate greatly between seasons. An increase appears in the 2010s, but several recent autumns are less well supplied. These variations must also be understood in the context of dispersed migration, only part of which passes within view of the watchpoint.

### Distinctive observations

An Osprey has been seen migrating with a fish in its talons. Passage can offer glimpses of behaviour as well as opportunities to count birds.

## European Honey Buzzard

### Passage at the Défilé

The Honey Buzzard is one of the raptors most regularly counted at the Défilé. Passage is concentrated around late August and early September. In fine weather, birds may travel high overhead, across the Jura or far to the east. Cloud covering the hills can instead funnel them through the Défilé and bring them closer to the observers.

### Changes in passage

Observed numbers vary greatly between autumns. Over 1993–2017, these fluctuations make a clear trend difficult to identify. Several recent seasons remain below the levels seen in the 2000s and early 2010s. Weather and visibility nevertheless matter: weak visible passage may reflect more dispersed migration, partly beyond the observers’ reach.

### Distinctive observations

Before migration begins, local Honey Buzzards may gather in a thermal, climb very high and then descend again. Such gatherings can give the impression that passage has already started. Conversely, genuinely migrating flocks have been found at the very limit of binocular visibility by systematically scanning the sky.

## Hen Harrier

### Passage at the Défilé

The Hen Harrier migrates on a broad front and is only weakly concentrated by the landscape. Birds may hunt or rest along the Jura ridges. Some winter relatively far north, so late movements associated with cold weather can escape monitoring that ends in November.

### Changes in passage

Passage declined markedly between the 1990s and the 2010s. Milder winters allowing birds to remain farther north, and declines in European breeding populations, are among the proposed explanations. Their respective contributions to weak local passage have not been established.

## Montagu's Harrier

### Passage at the Défilé

Montagu’s Harriers remain scarce at the Défilé, with numbers varying greatly between autumns. With so few birds, a handful of sightings can substantially affect comparisons. Confirmed identifications must also be distinguished from birds left in the “Pallid or Montagu’s Harrier” category.

## Western Marsh Harrier

### Passage at the Défilé

The Western Marsh Harrier migrates on a broad front and can cross hills without being funnelled through the Défilé. It combines powered flight with the use of rising air. Some birds stop at the Étournel wetlands, where they can feed before continuing their journey.

### Changes in passage

Passage has gone through several phases: strong seasons in the 1990s, a decline in the early 2000s and a recovery around 2010. Recent years remain variable, with particularly strong passage in 2025. These phases do not form a continuous increase.

## Pallid Harrier

### Passage at the Défilé

Once exceptional at the Défilé, the Pallid Harrier has become more regular. This forms part of an increase in sightings in western Europe. Better knowledge of identification features contributes to that change but does not fully explain it.

### Distinctive observations

Separation from Montagu’s Harrier can remain difficult, particularly for some young birds. Records left unidentified between the two species are therefore useful, rather than forcing an identification.

## Rough-legged Buzzard

### Passage at the Défilé

The Rough-legged Buzzard remains exceptional at the Défilé. A few isolated sightings punctuate the monitoring record, too few to establish a local passage trend.

## Common Buzzard

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common Buzzard is one of the large raptors characteristic of migration at the Défilé. Autumn passage can be reinforced by birds escaping cold, frost or snow, sometimes well beyond the usual monitoring period. Local birds complicate interpretation: they may climb in a thermal and then return to their territory rather than migrate.

### Changes in passage

The increase observed over 1993–2017 does not continue consistently through recent seasons, several of which show weak passage. Weather affects departure, routes and visibility alike. Numbers counted at the site therefore cannot be read directly as changes in breeding populations.

## Northern Nutcracker

### Distinctive observations

During monitoring in 1993, a few Spotted Nutcrackers appeared to be moving with Eurasian Jays. This remains an isolated observation rather than evidence of regular passage at the Défilé.

## Western Jackdaw

### Passage at the Défilé

The Western Jackdaw sometimes accompanies Rooks. Its passage has become much smaller than at the start of monitoring, although its share of mixed flocks can be higher when Rooks are particularly scarce.

### Changes in passage

The decline in migrants is not shared uniformly by neighbouring breeding populations. A decline in birds originating in Germany and an increasing tendency to remain near breeding areas are among the proposed explanations. A weak season at the Défilé therefore does not describe the species’ fortunes throughout the region.

## White Stork

### Passage at the Défilé

The White Stork is an emblematic August migrant. Flocks often pass several kilometres east of the watchpoint and rarely follow the Jura. Heat haze can conceal them, despite their size suggesting they should be easy to spot. Ring readings and tracking data show the importance of birds originating in Switzerland and Germany in particular.

### Changes in passage

Once scarce in the counts, the White Stork has increased strongly since the early 2000s. Growing breeding populations contribute, alongside better detection of distant flocks. Having several observers allows more attention to be given to the eastern sector. Passage also spread over a longer period between 1993 and 2019, beginning earlier and ending later.

### Distinctive observations

The Jura forms a barrier that storks rarely cross. Swiss birds therefore favour the Défilé de l’Écluse route, whereas those from Alsace mainly pass through Franche-Comté.

## Black Stork

### Passage at the Défilé

Less numerous than the White Stork, the Black Stork passes alone or in small groups, often on sunny days. Against a blue sky these birds can easily go unnoticed, especially away from large streams of raptors. Ring readings link birds crossing the Swiss Plateau notably with central Europe.

### Changes in passage

After a relatively stable period up to the mid-2000s, passage increased before becoming more variable in recent seasons. Between the 1990s and the late 2010s, observed migration began earlier and extended over a longer period. This local change fits a context of expanding western European breeding populations, rather than a uniform increase throughout Europe.

## Short-toed Snake Eagle

### Passage at the Défilé

The Short-toed Snake Eagle was long an irregular visitor to the Défilé before becoming more regular from the late 2000s onwards. The expansion of its breeding range in Haute-Savoie and Switzerland provides context for this change. The presence of regional birds nevertheless makes it necessary to distinguish local movements from true migration.

## Rook

### Passage at the Défilé

The Rook formerly passed in large flocks, sometimes in long processions of tightly packed birds. It may travel with Western Jackdaws. In poor weather, flocks land in fields at the foot of the Vuache before continuing when conditions improve.

### Changes in passage

The collapse in passage since the large movements of the 1970s and 1980s is one of the site’s most striking changes. It appears to reflect wintering closer to breeding areas, farther east in Europe, more than a decline in breeding birds. Milder winters may favour this reduced migration. Routes on the other side of the Jura have not experienced the same decline.

## Eurasian Whimbrel

### Distinctive observations

Whimbrel records also include birds stopping over. In 2010, very unsettled weather brought an unusual arrival of grounded birds, making local passage exceptional. Such an episode does not necessarily indicate a general increase in the migration flow.

## Common Kestrel

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common Kestrel migrates on a broad front and can pass quickly, without being strongly concentrated by the landscape. Local birds hunting around the site complicate migrant counts. Low cloud and some winds can make passage more visible by bringing birds closer to observers.

### Changes in passage

Passage increased from the early 2010s, but the scale of the change is not fully explained. Recent years remain variable. Detection conditions need to be considered alongside this pattern, particularly for a small raptor capable of passing far from the watchpoint.

## Eurasian Hobby

### Passage at the Défilé

The Eurasian Hobby is only weakly funnelled by the landscape and can cross the Mediterranean on a broad front. At the Défilé, numbers remain modest and fast-moving birds can easily escape notice. Local variations therefore reflect only part of its migration.

### Changes in passage

After fluctuations with no clear trend up to 2017, several recent seasons are weak. Autumn 2025 stands out against this run with exceptionally strong passage. The contrast illustrates how much the usually small numbers can vary between years.

## Red-footed Falcon

### Passage at the Défilé

The Red-footed Falcon is rare on autumn migration at the Défilé. Its loop migration takes many birds farther east at this season. A year rich in local sightings, such as 2025, is therefore a distinctive event rather than typical passage.

## Peregrine Falcon

### Passage at the Défilé

The Peregrine Falcon is seen both as a local bird and as a migrant. Regional birds may hunt passing migrants, while Peregrines from farther north continue towards their winter quarters. The small number identified as migrants makes a trend difficult to establish.

## Merlin

### Passage at the Défilé

The Merlin often passes fast and low, sometimes close to woodland, making it easy to miss. Some birds stop to hunt. The small number of migrants detected needs to be understood in light of this inconspicuous passage, rather than as a complete measure of the flow.

## Eurasian Jay

### Passage at the Défilé

The Eurasian Jay is distinctive for its irruptions, with large movements in some autumns. Birds follow wooded areas, move on broad fronts and may also use Alpine passes. The watchpoint therefore captures only part of these movements.

### Changes in passage

Several irruptions occurred over 1993–2019, with very different intensities at the Défilé. These movements are linked to poor acorn crops in northern regions combined with good breeding success. They do not necessarily reach France, or may involve only small numbers there. The scale of a northern European irruption is therefore not always reflected in local counts.

### Distinctive observations

Fog can sometimes help observation: in 2012 it appears to have brought jays closer to the watchpoint, making them easier to find and count.

## Yellow-legged Gull

### Passage at the Défilé

The Yellow-legged Gull makes many movements between Lake Geneva and the Rhône, in both directions. These local flights mix with an autumn movement towards the south-west, probably associated with birds leaving the lake after summer. Separating migration from regional movements therefore remains difficult.

## Great Cormorant

### Passage at the Défilé

The Great Cormorant is particularly active early in the morning. Large flocks follow the Rhône or cross neighbouring hills. Movements between roosts and fishing grounds nevertheless complicate interpretation: not every south-westbound flight is necessarily a definitive departure.

### Changes in passage

Passage increased in the 2010s, with large fluctuations and more variable recent seasons. Expanding breeding populations provide part of the context. More regular coverage from dawn also improves detection of the earliest flocks and may contribute to the appearance of an earlier start to passage.

## Great Egret

### Passage at the Défilé

The Great Egret migrates mainly by day, making its passage more accessible to visual monitoring than that of many other herons. It may travel with Grey Herons. Its east-to-west movements are not necessarily concentrated in the Défilé funnel, however: some birds can cross the Jura directly.

### Changes in passage

Very rare in the early 1990s, the Great Egret gradually became a regular and much more numerous migrant. This transformation accompanies the species’ expansion in Europe. Protection, changes in food resources and increasing use of fields in autumn and winter form part of the context for this growth.

### Distinctive observations

Once heavily hunted for its feathers, the Great Egret benefited greatly from protection. Ring recoveries suggest a mainly Hungarian, and probably Austrian, origin for some birds passing through Haute-Savoie.

## Common Crane

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common Crane migrates by day and by night and readily crosses moderate hills such as the Jura. Visible passage can be highly concentrated, but part of the flow escapes daytime monitoring. Differences between years therefore also reflect how much migration takes place within view and during observation hours.

### Changes in passage

Almost incidental at the start of monitoring, passage grew substantially in the 2010s. This accompanied the development of a secondary route through central Europe, north of the Alps and across the Swiss Plateau, against a background of growing European populations. Numbers have since remained very variable between autumns.

## European Bee-eater

### Passage at the Défilé

The European Bee-eater became a regular migrant at the Défilé from the 2010s, after a long period of irregular occurrence. Passage can come in several waves. More northerly populations, notably in Germany, leave their breeding grounds later than southern populations and can therefore contribute a second movement.

### Changes in passage

Increasing sightings accompany an expansion of breeding in Switzerland and Germany. Warming offers one possible explanation, but colony protection and the creation of suitable breeding habitat have also contributed to this growth. Annual fluctuations remain substantial at the Défilé.

## Common Merganser

### Passage at the Défilé

Common Mergansers seen at the Défilé are not always easy to classify as migrants: some movements may involve dispersal along the Rhône. Increasing observations in the early 2010s also fit a context of regional expansion.

## Western House Martin

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common House Martin crosses the Défilé within movements involving several hirundine species. Concentrations can be large, but birds are not always identified individually. Totals combining all hirundines therefore do not describe this species’ passage on their own.

## Sand Martin

### Passage at the Défilé

The Sand Martin is seen among flocks of Barn Swallows and Common House Martins. Some early accounts describe passage for these species together rather than separately. This shared basis for observations needs to be kept in mind when interpreting differences between years.

## Eurasian Crag Martin

### Passage at the Défilé

The Eurasian Crag Martin forms only a small share of hirundines seen at the Défilé. It can nevertheless occur very late in autumn, into the final days of monitoring. Totals combining all hirundines should not be attributed to this species, which is scarce in the counts.

## swallow sp.

### Passage at the Défilé

Hirundines can pass in mixed flocks that are too distant or numerous for every bird to be identified. The unidentified category retains this part of passage. It needs to be considered when comparing numbers assigned to Barn Swallows, Common House Martins and Sand Martins.

## Barn Swallow

### Passage at the Défilé

The Barn Swallow takes part in the large hirundine movements seen at the Défilé, alongside Common House Martins and Sand Martins. Flocks can be very numerous and difficult to count separately. In early monitoring, some passage was estimated by sampling, calling for care in historical comparisons.

## Grey Heron

### Passage at the Défilé

The Grey Heron migrates by day and by night. At the Défilé, the largest flocks are often detected at dawn or dusk, although birds also pass in full daylight. Alone, in small groups or in large flocks, they may cross quite high above the site.

### Changes in passage

Counts rose strongly from the late 2010s, with very substantial passage in recent seasons. Extending observation into the first and last hours of daylight helps capture migration that is partly nocturnal. This improved coverage needs to be considered before attributing the entire increase to population growth.

## Common Swift

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common Swift is an early migrant whose passage can already be substantial in summer. Birds sometimes cross at every height visible through binoculars and telescopes. This vertical spread makes counting difficult and can produce a marked contrast between spectacular passage days and much quieter ones.

## Alpine Swift

### Passage at the Défilé

The Alpine Swift appears in small numbers in the available observations. The presence of local breeding birds sometimes makes true migrants difficult to recognise.

## Black Kite

### Passage at the Défilé

The Black Kite is an early migrant: much of its passage takes place in July, before monitoring begins at many other sites in August. Birds use thermals to gain height. Strengthening winds or a thunderstorm can interrupt their progress and force them to land.

### Changes in passage

Passage increased markedly between 1993 and 2017, despite a decline around the turn of the century. This probably reflects, in part, growth in the breeding populations of Switzerland and Germany. Recent seasons are more varied, with exceptional passage in some years and much smaller movements in others; 2025 falls within the average of the preceding decade.

### Distinctive observations

Migration can continue very late in the day. In 2025, kites were repeatedly seen migrating shortly before sunset, and one flock passed after sunset, outside the counting protocol.

## Red Kite

### Passage at the Défilé

The Red Kite is one of the Défilé’s emblematic species. Birds use thermals, but their movements are not confined to the warmest hours: migrants pass early in the morning and late in the day, sometimes even in rain or headwinds. Cold spells can trigger further movements after autumn monitoring has ended.

### Changes in passage

Passage has grown strongly since the 1990s, with a particularly marked acceleration from 2011 onwards. This accompanies growth notably in the Swiss and German populations. Strong recent seasons continue that pattern, alongside fluctuations between autumns. Some late passage may escape monitoring when cold weather arrives after the season closes.

## Mediterranean Gull

### Passage at the Défilé

The Mediterranean Gull remains infrequent in the Défilé observations. Birds may accompany Black-headed Gulls, as did the young birds that characterised the strong 2024 season. Regional sightings and birds identified as migrants do not always represent the same kind of movement.

## Black-headed Gull

### Passage at the Défilé

Black-headed Gull movements are not always easy to interpret. Flocks gain height, descend again or depart in different directions. Some eventually form triangular formations and continue south-west, giving a clearer indication of migration.

## Eurasian Blue Tit

### Passage at the Défilé

The Eurasian Blue Tit moves mainly during irruptions that vary greatly between autumns. It may progress slowly from bush to bush and migrate on a broad front. Lowland routes appear important, but a movement visible at an Alpine pass may also go undetected at the Défilé.

### Changes in passage

Up to 2019, irruptions became more frequent in local observations. In northern regions, good breeding success and years of poor food availability can prompt many young birds to leave. The scale of these movements nevertheless varies with the routes used.

## Great Tit

### Passage at the Défilé

The Great Tit shows irruptions rather than abundant passage every year. Depending on the autumn, movements may be more visible in lowlands or at Alpine passes. Its late appearance in the Défilé counts therefore does not mean that such movements are new.

### Distinctive observations

The availability of beechmast and hazelnuts is among the factors associated with these irruptions. Unlike those of the Eurasian Blue Tit, they were not attributed to growth in northern populations over the period up to 2019.

## Coal Tit

### Passage at the Défilé

The Coal Tit forms only a small part of tit movements seen at the Défilé. It prefers to travel through habitats containing conifers, which are absent immediately around the watchpoint. Major irruptions at mountain passes can therefore be only faintly apparent here.

## Stock Dove

### Passage at the Défilé

The Stock Dove mixes with Common Wood Pigeon flocks, usually in much smaller numbers. Identifying it requires favourable viewing distance and light. Flocks that are too distant or tightly packed may remain in the unidentified-pigeon category, limiting comparisons between seasons.

### Changes in passage

Its share among identified pigeons increased in the 2000s and early 2010s, partly because Common Wood Pigeon passage declined. That share subsequently fell, alongside a decline in Stock Doves themselves. Changes in proportion therefore do not necessarily mean an increase in Stock Dove numbers.

## Columba sp.

### Passage at the Défilé

This category groups pigeons that cannot be confidently identified because of distance, light or flock density. It is essential to understanding comparisons between Common Wood Pigeons and Stock Doves: changes in identified birds may also reflect observation conditions or the way flocks are recorded.

## Common Wood Pigeon

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common Wood Pigeon crosses the region on a broad north-east to south-west front. Passage waves can be highly concentrated in October. Stock Doves regularly mix with the flocks, and distance or flock density can make separation impossible. Depending on the year and recording practices, a variable share of birds is therefore recorded as unidentified pigeons.

### Changes in passage

Passage declined greatly after the large movements of the 1960s and 1970s, without a corresponding general decline of the species in Europe. Reduced movements by some central European populations, milder winters and hunting are among the proposed explanations. Routes on the other side of the Jura can also show different patterns. Exceptional passage in 2025 contrasts sharply with the weak seasons of several decades.

### Distinctive observations

Weather can alter flock routes: low cloud sometimes favours passage through the Défilé or over the Vuache, whereas clear skies allow birds to cross the Jura. Flocks may also follow wooded slopes and take refuge there when attacked by a raptor.

## Eurasian Chaffinch

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common Chaffinch is among the most numerous small passerines in visible passage. Early monitoring estimated part of these flows by sampling. The apparent scale of passage therefore also needs to be understood in terms of counting methods and the attention devoted to small birds.

## White-tailed Eagle

### Passage at the Défilé

The White-tailed Eagle remains a rare encounter in passage observations. Recent records must also take account of birds from the Lake Geneva reintroduction programme: an appearance in the area does not necessarily involve a long-distance migrant.

## Great White Pelican

### Distinctive observations

In 2008, an adult Great White Pelican accompanied a flock of White Storks. Its journey was subsequently followed as far as north-eastern Spain. This encounter illustrates the rare surprises that watching large migrant flocks can bring.

## Caspian Tern

### Passage at the Défilé

The Caspian Tern is the largest tern. An uncommon passage bird in France, it is seen only occasionally at the Défilé. Some gull and tern migration takes place at night, further limiting what observers at the watchpoint can detect.

## Wallcreeper

### Distinctive observations

The Wallcreeper is also known as a regular winter visitor to the walls and cliffs of Fort l’Écluse itself. A sighting in the area may therefore relate to a local winter presence rather than a major migration flow.

## Griffon Vulture

### Passage at the Défilé

The Griffon Vulture is an infrequent visitor to the Défilé. Birds spending summer in the Prealps may remain far from the watchpoint before heading south in autumn. The few observed passages therefore represent only part of the regional movements.

## Black-winged Kite

### Passage at the Défilé

The Black-winged Kite first appears in the Défilé records in 2014 and becomes more regular in the 2020s. Sightings remain few. They form part of the species’ movements and expansion northwards and eastwards within Europe.

## Eurasian Sparrowhawk

### Passage at the Défilé

Eurasian Sparrowhawks pass alone, in small groups or among buzzards. Detection depends greatly on conditions: high or distant birds can easily be missed, whereas some winds bring them closer to the watchpoint. Their presence within a stream of larger raptors can also help observers find them.

### Changes in passage

Following an increase from the late 2000s into the early 2010s, counts declined before becoming more variable. The recovery seen in 2025 illustrates these fluctuations. Differences in visibility and routes call for caution before interpreting them as direct changes in populations.

## Common Starling

### Passage at the Défilé

The Common Starling can pass in large, highly concentrated waves. The 2013 and 2014 seasons contrasted strongly with the much smaller movements of preceding years. A few episodes can therefore give an autumn’s passage a very distinctive character.
